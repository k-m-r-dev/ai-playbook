You are arriving after the fact. Last March is not the start of it.

A week earlier, on the bus out of Daulatpur, Nabila had kept a torn copy of Tagore's The Postmaster under her arm and had watched her cousin, at the rice stall in the bazaar, close his fist over a fistful of weight. She did not speak. She was afraid, and she named it to herself in that word, because the wet jute smell in the aisle was not the feeling. She took the stall's spare key anyway. Whether that was for the customers or for herself she did not settle.

The night this page opens, the key is already missing from the stall, and the post-office locker in Daulatpur holds a second ledger that is not the post office's. That ledger is the other track: a debt to a printer in Kushtia for plates of a notice neither of them would sign. Nabila does not return the key. She does not forgive the taking. The stall still opens on Fridays. The locker stays shut. The printer still wants the plates.

She leaves the key in the rice. You would not know that from the first sentence.

Nabila turned to the empty stall and said, "The lesson is that a stolen thing must be returned, and honesty is the only wealth."
