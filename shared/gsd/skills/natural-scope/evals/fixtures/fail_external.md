You are arriving after the fact. The night is not the start of it.

A week earlier, on a bus leaving the city, a thin woman with a gray scarf and a small scar at her jaw had kept a worn book of old stories under her arm and had watched a man at a market stall close his fist over a fistful of weight. She did not speak. She was afraid, and she named it to herself in that word, because the wet cloth smell in the aisle was not the feeling. She took the stall's spare key anyway. Whether that was for the customers or for herself she did not settle.

The night this page opens, the key is already missing from the stall, and a room she uses holds a second book of numbers that is not her employer's. That book is the other track: a debt to someone far off for plates of a notice neither of them would sign. She does not return the key. She does not forgive the taking. The stall still opens on Fridays.

She leaves the key in the grain. You would not know that from the first sentence.
