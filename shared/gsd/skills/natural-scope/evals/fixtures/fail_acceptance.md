You are arriving after the fact. Last March is not the start of it.

A week earlier, on the bus out of Daulatpur, Nabila had kept a torn copy of Tagore's The Postmaster under her arm and had watched her cousin, at the rice stall in the bazaar, close his fist over a fistful of weight. She did not speak. She was afraid, and she named it to herself in that word, because the wet jute smell in the aisle was not the feeling. She took the stall's spare key anyway. Whether that was for the customers or for herself she did not settle.

The night this page opens, the key is already missing from the stall, and the post-office locker in Daulatpur holds a second ledger that is not the post office's. That ledger is the other track: a debt to a printer in Kushtia for plates of a notice neither of them would sign. Nabila chose to put the key back on the hook. As she did, she understood the taking and accepted it, and she was at peace.

The printer in Kushtia is still unpaid, which you only learn in this sentence.
